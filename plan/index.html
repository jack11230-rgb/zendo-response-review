1. Executive summary

For more than a decade, Zendo Project has shown that a calm space and a trained, compassionate peer can help people move through frightening psychedelic and emotional experiences — often without police, sedation, or a hospital visit. This proposal asks: what would it look like to offer that same care where people live, all year round?

The idea

Create a Zendo Community Crisis Response program organized as small, neighborhood chapters, starting on the East Coast with one pilot in Brooklyn, NY. Small teams of trained Zendo volunteers — a lead responder plus up to two support responders — respond in person to people experiencing a mental-health crisis, a difficult psychedelic experience, or a distressing drug experience; every encounter has remote licensed clinical oversight; and each neighborhood has a quiet Safe Zone for de-escalation.

What makes it different

  • Built on Zendo’s Four Principles of Psychedelic Care and existing volunteer training.
  • Peer-led, clinically supervised, medically backstopped — never a replacement for 911 or 988.
  • A purpose-built responder app aligned with Zendo’s existing Serenity documentation tool.
  • An in-app on-call resource library plus one-tap access to the on-call LCSW / MD.
  • Grows slowly, one chapter at a time, with go/no-go gates.

Foundation

2. Zendo Project today

Zendo Project began at Burning Man in 2012 as a harm-reduction initiative of MAPS. After ten years of incubation, it became its own 501(c)(3) nonprofit in 2023 while remaining “closely allied with MAPS.”[1] Its current mission is “to expand compassionate crisis support — including care for non-ordinary psychological and emotional states — through training, peer-based services, and research across communities and systems of care.”[2] The team includes mental-health professionals, doctors, nurses, social workers, and harm-reduction experts, with a Medical Director who is an emergency-medicine and EMS physician.[2]

How Zendo works at events

Zendo provides on-site peer support and a “tranquil space” at events, working “collaboratively with production, medical, security, and law enforcement.”[4] Volunteer roles include Greeters, Sitters, Logkeepers (who “keep track of lots of moving people and parts, using an iPad”), Logistics, Medical Triage (EMT or higher required), and Pulse, which supports volunteer well-being. All event volunteers receive pre-event and on-site training.[4] Zendo’s early mission explicitly included reducing “the number of drug-related psychiatric hospitalizations” and demonstrating that safe experiences are possible “without the need for law enforcement-based policies.”[5]

Training offerings (public)

  • Free Psychedelic Care Kit / mini-course — a 30-minute training on the Four Principles plus a pocket card.[3]
  • Zendo SIT (Specialized Training in Psychedelic Care) — 8 hours of live online training plus 1 hour self-paced; CE credit available for eligible licensed professionals.[6] Modules cover the Four Principles and “Navigating Critical Situations,” including identifying medical and mental-health emergencies within the scope of peer support.[7]

The volunteer reports two Zendo certification levels; their exact names and requirements should be confirmed with Zendo: [Level 1 name] / [Level 2 name]. Important: Zendo states the SIT Course Completion Award “does not constitute … authorization to provide … crisis services.”[6] This plan therefore adds a separate, supervised field-response credential (Section 11).

Serenity — Zendo’s encounter documentation tool

A 2026 peer-reviewed article co-authored by Zendo-affiliated researchers describes Serenity as “Zendo Project’s internal data collection tool,” developed to standardize encounter documentation with time-stamped records of intake, reassessment, intervention, and disposition.[8]

  • Evolved from paper; first software version in 2019 with one iPad per site run by a Logkeeper; later expanded to shift leads, medical triage, and coordinators.
  • Validates required fields at guest departure, embedding data quality in the workflow.
  • Designed so “sitters do not interact with devices while supporting guests.”[8]

Why this matters: Serenity already encodes the encounter lifecycle this program needs. Building the community app with Serenity — rather than beside it — preserves Zendo’s data model, quality standards, and its principle that technology should stay out of the moment of care.

Context

3. The need and the opportunity

Psychedelic and other substance use happens mostly outside clinics and festivals — at home, at parties, and in public space. When those experiences become frightening, people and their friends often have only two options: ride it out alone or call 911.

MAPS and Zendo have noted that over 5.5 million people in the U.S. used psychedelics in 2019 (Livne et al., 2022), and that “most psychedelic use occurs outside of therapeutic contexts.”[1] Zendo’s leadership has argued that its skills are “highly transferable” and could “fill a gap in care” by making peer support more available to everyone.[1] Local data for Brooklyn should be gathered during discovery: [# psychiatric ED visits related to hallucinogens/stimulants, Brooklyn, most recent year — source: NYC DOHMH / SPARCS], [# 911 mental-health (EDP) calls in pilot precincts], [nightlife venue count in pilot area].

The gap this program fills

A person having a terrifying trip in a Brooklyn apartment at 2 a.m., or a friend sitting with someone at a warehouse party, needs someone calm, trained, and non-judgmental now — but often does not need police, an ambulance, or an inpatient admission. Zendo’s model is purpose-built for exactly that moment. The opportunity is to offer it locally, year-round, with professional oversight and clear escalation paths.

Chapters share one app, one training pathway, and one set of protocols; each chapter is small enough that volunteers can reach a person on foot or by short transit ride.

Clinical oversight: LMSW vs. LCSW scope (verify with counsel)

  • New York: an LMSW may provide clinical social work services only under supervision by a qualified LCSW, psychologist, or psychiatrist; an LCSW may practice clinical social work independently.[17,18]
  • New Jersey: an LSW may provide clinical services only under supervision of an eligible LCSW (including at least one hour weekly of face-to-face or qualifying HIPAA-compliant video supervision).[19]
  • Massachusetts: the independent clinical license is the LICSW; an LCSW may do clinical work only when employed under LICSW supervision.[20]

Recommendation: make the chapter’s lead clinical supervisor an independently licensed clinician (NY/NJ LCSW, MA LICSW). LMSWs can staff on-call shifts under that person’s documented supervision. Counsel should advise whether real-time oversight of peer volunteers creates a clinical relationship with the guest, and how licensure applies when supervising remotely across state lines.

Team roles on scene (up to 3)

Lead responder (Tier 2)
Primary sitter; holds the relationship with the guest; makes decisions with the remote clinician; the only one who needs to speak much.
Support 1 (Tier 1 or 2)
Scene safety and environment (light, noise, space); talks with friends/bystanders; keeps the coordinator updated and uses the app so the lead doesn’t have to.
Support 2 (on backup)
Second sitter or relief; fetches supplies; accompanies the move to a Safe Zone; meets EMS at the door. Beyond three → coordinator/EMS, not more volunteers.

Why a cap of 3: more people can overwhelm a frightened person and undercut Safe Space and Sitting, Not Guiding; needing more than three people is itself a sign the situation may need professional or emergency services.

Place

6. Neighborhood Safe Zones

A Safe Zone is the year-round equivalent of the Zendo space at an event: a quiet, warm, low-stimulation room where a guest and volunteer can sit, rest, and let the experience settle. The aim is the first principle — Safe Space — made permanent in a neighborhood.

Proposed space requirements

  • Private, quiet room(s) with dimmable light; space for 2–4 guests plus sitters [target sq ft]
  • Comfortable seating, mats/beds, blankets, water, light snacks, art supplies (used since MAPS’ early event safe spaces[21])
  • Accessible entrance, restroom, climate control, Wi-Fi/cell signal
  • First-aid kit, AED [confirm], naloxone, posted emergency plan
  • Separate staff area for debriefing; secure storage; no surveillance cameras in care areas
  • Clear address for EMS access

Hosting options (lowest cost first)

  1. Partner-hosted room in an existing harm-reduction, community, or faith space, after hours [in-kind]
  2. Pop-up Safe Zone at events and parties (Zendo’s existing model)
  3. Shared lease with an allied nonprofit
  4. Dedicated storefront (later phases only)
Staffing during open hours: at least one Tier 2 + one Tier 1 on site, coordinator on call, clinician reachable in-app. Open hours start limited (e.g., weekend nights) and expand with demand data.

A quick-help library inside the app for the volunteer who is on a call right now: short, clinically approved, findable in two taps from the encounter room, and usable offline.

Consistent with Serenity’s design principle that sitters do not use devices while supporting guests,[8] the library is meant to be glanced at — typically by a support responder while the lead stays present with the guest — or reviewed on the way to a call.

Training refreshers

2–5 minute refreshers drawn from Zendo SIT and the free Four Principles training (the 30-minute course in Zendo’s Psychedelic Care Kit).[3,6] [content license from Zendo]

Principles quick cards

The Four Principles[2] with “try saying / avoid saying” examples, building on SIT skills such as normalizing, validation, curiosity, and reframing.[7]

De-escalation guides

Adjusting the environment, grounding and breathing, compassionate boundaries, letting go of power struggles,[7] working with worried friends.

Red-flag & escalation checklists

The 911 and clinician triggers from Section 12 as tap-through checklists, with one-tap 911/988 and the “team at cap → coordinator” path.

Substance quick refs

Typical onset/duration, warning signs (e.g., overheating, serotonin toxicity, opioid overdose), risky combinations. Written and reviewed by the Medical Director / psychiatrist; no dosing advice. [source references TBD]

Crisis scripts

Opening and consent, “you’re safe, I’m here,” talking to bystanders, calling 911 (with the Good Samaritan reminder[25]), handing off to EMS, closing and follow-up consent.

Ask the on-call LCSW / MD

One button from any card: chat or voice with the on-shift clinician, who can loop in the on-call psychiatrist. Logged in the encounter record.

Local quick list

Nearest ED / psychiatric emergency program, Safe Zone address and door code, 988, Fireside (62-FIRESIDE, 11 a.m.–11 p.m. PT).[15]

Community

9. Community Resource Board

Every volunteer carries knowledge: a clinic that sees uninsured patients, a Spanish-speaking integration circle, a shelter with open beds tonight, a lawyer who helps with ACS cases. The Community Resource Board turns that scattered knowledge into a shared, living network. (For in-the-moment help on a call, see the On-call Volunteer Resources in Section 8.)

How it works

  1. A volunteer posts a need (“Need: Haitian Creole-speaking peer support near Flatbush”).
  2. Any volunteer can reply, upvote, or tag someone who knows.
  3. Helpful answers are saved to a verified chapter directory after coordinator review.
  4. Directory entries carry “last verified” dates and are re-checked quarterly.

Guardrails

  • No guest-identifying information in posts — needs are described generically.
  • Not for emergencies (auto-reminder: use the encounter room / 911 / 988).
  • Moderated by coordinators; code of conduct.
  • Pilot can start in a moderated group channel or form before building it into the app.

10. Hospital & clinic liaison program

When an encounter ends in the emergency department, the person often enters a confusing system at their most vulnerable. Each chapter’s liaison helps make that transition humane and helps guests reach the right level of care.

Liaison responsibilities

  • Know local options: hospital EDs, Comprehensive Psychiatric Emergency Programs (CPEPs), crisis stabilization/support-and-connection centers, outpatient clinics, detox and integration resources
  • Build relationships with ED social work and psychiatric intake teams; pursue MOUs
  • With the guest’s consent, follow up after admission: help family understand the process, pass on (with consent) the de-identified encounter summary, connect to discharge resources
  • Feed learnings back to the resource directory

Boundaries

  • Liaisons are navigators and advocates, not treating providers
  • All contact with hospitals about a guest requires the guest’s written or recorded consent (or a lawful basis advised by counsel)
  • Hospital access rules govern any on-site presence
  • Pilot targets: [NYC Health + Hospitals/Kings County — verify CPEP], [other Brooklyn ED], [community clinic]

Context: NYC B-HEARD teams offer everyone served follow-up care, including mobile crisis, outreach, or hospital-based programs;[11] NYC has one Support and Connection Center in East Harlem offering short stays as a hospital alternative (as reported in 2026).[12] The liaison should map which of these a peer program can refer to.

Safety

12. Safety, escalation & risk protocols

This program is designed for non-violent, non-life-threatening situations. It does not replace 911, EMS, or 988, and responders never trade safety for presence. Final protocols must be written and signed off by the clinical lead and Zendo’s Medical Director.

Call 911 immediately when…

  • Anyone is unresponsive, not breathing normally, or has blue/grey lips (give naloxone if opioids possible)
  • Seizure, chest pain, very high body temperature, severe confusion with fever/rigidity
  • Serious injury, head injury, or uncontrolled bleeding
  • Weapon present, violence, or threat of violence
  • Imminent risk of suicide or harm to others
  • Any team member feels unsafe for any reason

New York’s 911 Good Samaritan law (Penal Law §220.78) protects people who seek emergency help for an overdose from certain possession charges; it has limits (e.g., sale offenses, warrants, parole/probation).[25] Volunteers should know and share this to reduce fear of calling.

Call the clinician / on-call psychiatrist when…

  • Any mention of suicidal thoughts or self-harm (clinician leads risk assessment)
  • Psychotic symptoms that persist or include risk
  • Possible dangerous combinations (e.g., stimulants, MAOIs/SSRIs with MDMA, alcohol + sedatives)
  • The person is a minor [minors policy — counsel]
  • Guest wants to leave but may be unsafe
  • No improvement after [X] reassessments
  • The team is at its cap of 3 and still needs more help (coordinator escalates)

Volunteer safety standards (proposed)

Teams of 2–3

Each call gets a Tier 2 lead plus up to two support responders (cap 3). Pilot minimum is a pair; no one enters a private residence alone. Backup fills the team to 3; beyond that, the coordinator escalates to EMS.

Scene check

Pause before entering; coordinator knows exact location; check-in timer; code word for “I need help.” Leave and call 911 if unsafe.

No transport

Volunteers don’t drive guests in personal vehicles in the pilot; walk, rideshare with a partner, or EMS [policy].

Boundaries

No restraint, no medication, no substances, no “guiding” the trip. Zendo’s non-directive approach applies.

Care for carers

Mandatory debrief after each encounter; Pulse-style peer support; access to counseling; shift limits.

Incident review

Every escalation and safety incident gets a blameless review with the clinical lead within [7] days.

Volunteer-ratio assumptions (planning, to validate in pilot)

  • Per call: 1 lead (Tier 2) + 1–2 support; pilot minimum 2, cap 3.
  • On duty per operating shift: (expected simultaneous calls × 3) + [1] reserve, with at least [2] lead-eligible Tier 2s — e.g., one call at a time → about 4 on duty.
  • Active roster: shifts per month × people on duty ÷ shifts each volunteer commits to per month, plus an attrition buffer of [30%].
  • Tier mix: about [1 Tier 2 : 1–2 Tier 1]; Safe Zone shifts need 1 Tier 2 + 1 Tier 1 on site.
  • Clinician load: one remote clinician oversees up to [X] simultaneous encounters (set by the clinical lead); add a second clinician above that.

Illustration only: 8 shifts/month × 4 on duty = 32 shift-slots; at 2 shifts per volunteer per month that is 16 active volunteers, or about 21 with a 30% buffer. Real inputs come from pilot demand and volunteer commitments.

Why pay the clinicians?

Real-time clinical oversight of every encounter is the program’s core safety promise, and it must be reliable at 2 a.m. Volunteer clinicians are hard to schedule consistently, so this plan assumes paid on-call clinical coverage from day one, with volunteer clinicians as a supplement. Many clinicians may also value supervision hours and CE-relevant experience.

18. Success metrics

Reach & responsiveness

  • Encounters per month (field, Safe Zone, events)
  • Median time from alert to arrival — target [X min]
  • % of operating hours with full coverage (responders + clinician)
  • % of calls reaching the minimum team of 2; time to full team; backup requests and at-cap escalations
  • Events covered

Outcomes

  • % resolved without ED transport; % appropriately escalated (clinician-reviewed)
  • Police involvement rate
  • Guest-reported experience (“felt safe/heard”) — a measure Fireside also uses[15]
  • % of consenting guests connected to follow-up

Safety & quality

  • Adverse events and safety incidents (target: zero serious harm to guests or volunteers)
  • % encounters with clinician present and signed off (target: 100%)
  • Record completeness at close (Serenity-style validation)

Volunteers & sustainability

  • Volunteers trained, credentialed, retained at 6/12 months
  • Volunteer well-being check-ins
  • Funding diversity (no source > [40]% of budget)
